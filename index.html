Student eligibility

Who this program is for

We are looking for DC high school juniors who are curious about STEM, take challenging courses, and are preparing for an Ivy League or other highly selective college. Every student must also be an eligible participant in DC's Summer Youth Employment Program (SYEP).

1

A current high school junior

You are in 11th grade this school year and will be a rising senior in summer 2027.

2

Attending a DC high school

You attend a DC public or public charter high school. Students from schools with STEM programs and college-prep schools are especially encouraged to apply.

3

A demonstrated interest in STEM

You can show it through your coursework, a STEM program or academy, clubs, competitions, research, internships or projects you have built.

4

Preparing for a four-year college

You plan to attend a four-year college or university and have shown preparation toward an Ivy League education: rigorous classes, strong grades, and goals you can talk about.

5

An eligible SYEP student

You must apply to the Mayor Marion S. Barry Summer Youth Employment Program (SYEP) and be found eligible through the DC Department of Employment Services. Secure your SYEP eligibility before the summer begins. Students who are not SYEP-eligible cannot take part.

Required · Do this first

Secure your SYEP eligibility

SYEP is DC's summer jobs program for residents ages 14 to 24. Your SYEP application is separate from this program's application, and both must be complete.

  1. Apply at summerjobs.dc.gov while the application is open. It usually opens in late January and closes in early March (in 2026 it ran January 26 to March 6).
  2. Upload your eligibility documents, including proof of DC residency, proof of age, your Social Security number, proof that you can work in the United States, and parent consent if you are under 18.
  3. Check your SYEP account until your eligibility is confirmed, and fix any document that is flagged.
  4. Tell your counselor and The Chart Academy once you are found eligible.

Check summerjobs.dc.gov for the official 2027 dates and the full document checklist.

Parent involvement

Choosing a college is a family decision

Students make stronger college choices when the adults closest to them see the campuses too. That is why one parent or guardian may join their student on the Ivy League college tour.

Paid by the family

What parents cover

  • Your own meals during the tour
  • Any personal spending, souvenirs or extra activities
  • Time away from work for the week

Why your involvement matters

See it for yourself

Walking a campus answers questions a brochure cannot: how far it is from home, how students live, what support is available.

Talk about cost early

Ivy League schools offer generous need-based financial aid. Hearing it from admissions and financial aid staff helps families plan with real numbers.

Support the application

Senior fall moves fast. Parents who know the timeline can help with deadlines, financial aid forms and decisions in the spring.

Questions parents ask

Who can join the tour?

One parent or legal guardian per student. Let us know early so we can plan bus seats and hotel rooms.

Do I have to come?

No. Parent participation is optional. Students who travel without a parent are supervised by program staff at all times, and all staff are background-checked.

Does my student need to be in SYEP?

Yes. Every student must apply to DC's Summer Youth Employment Program at summerjobs.dc.gov and be found eligible. Students under 18 need a parent's consent, so please help your student gather their documents and finish the SYEP application before it closes.

Where will we stay?

Each parent shares a hotel room with their own student. Students traveling without a parent room with other students of the same gender, with staff on the same floor.

What does my student need to do to attend the tour?

The tour is part of a six-week program. Students are expected to take part in the exposure weeks and the internship week before the tour, and in the application preparation that follows.

How do I stay updated?

Families receive a full itinerary, packing list and emergency contacts before the tour, plus updates during the trip.

Component 4 · Preparing to apply

Your path from campus tour to college application

After the tour, the real work begins. Follow these steps through senior fall. Check off each item as you finish it; your progress is saved in this browser.

0 of 0 done
Week 6 · July

Reflect and build your college list

Use what you saw on tour to build a balanced list: a few reach schools, several target schools, and at least two schools you are confident you will get into and can afford.

July – August

Know the admissions requirements

Each school posts what it requires on its admissions website. Requirements and testing policies differ by school and can change from year to year, so check every school on your list.

August 1

Start the Common App

The Common Application opens August 1. All five schools on the tour accept it. Get the core pieces drafted before school starts.

September

Secure your letters of recommendation

Most selective colleges ask for a counselor recommendation and two teacher letters, usually from 11th-grade teachers in core subjects. Ask in person, early, and make it easy for them.

October

Apply for financial aid

All five tour schools meet the full demonstrated financial need of admitted students. To be considered, families file aid forms on time.

November 1

Submit early applications

Early deadlines at these schools are typically November 1. Decide whether to apply early to one school, and submit well before the deadline.

January

Submit regular decision applications

Regular deadlines fall in early January. Finish supplemental essays in December and send mid-year grades through your counselor.

What goes into an application

Selective colleges read every part of the application to understand who you are, not only your grades.

Transcript

Your grades and the rigor of your courses from 9th through 11th grade, plus your senior schedule.

Personal essay

Your own story, in your own voice. Admissions officers want to hear how you think.

Supplemental essays

School-specific questions, often about why you want to attend and what you will study.

Activities list

Up to ten activities, including jobs, family responsibilities, summer programs and STEM projects.

Recommendations

Letters from your counselor and teachers who know your work and character.

Testing, where required

SAT or ACT scores according to each school's current policy.
